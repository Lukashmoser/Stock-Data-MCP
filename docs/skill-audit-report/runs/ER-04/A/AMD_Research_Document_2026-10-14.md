## 1. COMPANY OVERVIEW

### What AMD does

Advanced Micro Devices is a fabless semiconductor company that designs high-performance computing processors and the software around them. Its products include x86 CPUs (EPYC for servers, Ryzen for PCs), GPUs (Instinct accelerators for data-center AI and HPC, Radeon for gaming and workstations), adaptive and embedded devices (Versal, Zynq, Kintex and Virtex FPGAs and SoCs from the 2022 Xilinx acquisition), data processing units and AI networking (Pensando), and semi-custom system-on-chips for game consoles (Sony PlayStation 5, Microsoft Xbox Series X|S). Since the 2025 acquisition of ZT Systems, AMD also designs full rack-scale AI systems, sold under the "Helios" name, that combine Instinct GPUs, EPYC CPUs, Pensando networking and the ROCm software stack.

AMD owns no wafer fabs. Leading-edge wafers come mainly from Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC). Older-node wafers come from GlobalFoundries (the former AMD manufacturing arm, spun out in 2009) and others. Assembly, test and packaging, including the advanced 2.5D and 3D packaging behind chiplet designs, are handled by partners such as TSMC (CoWoS, SoIC), ASE, Amkor and AMD's long-standing back-end joint ventures with Tongfu Microelectronics in China and Malaysia.

### How AMD makes money

AMD earns almost all of its revenue from selling chips, with a growing share from accelerator boards and rack-level systems. Pricing is per unit. List prices range from under USD 100 for entry-level client CPUs to several thousand dollars for top-bin EPYC processors, and tens of thousands of dollars per Instinct GPU. A rack-scale Helios system is a multi-million-dollar purchase. Software (ROCm, Vitis, the AMD AI software stack) is mostly free, and its role is to make the hardware easier to adopt rather than to generate revenue. Semi-custom console revenue is priced per chip under long-term contracts, and the console makers fund part of the engineering through non-recurring engineering (NRE) payments.

AMD reports four segments: **Data Center** (EPYC, Instinct, Pensando, rack-scale systems, and data-center FPGAs), **Client** (Ryzen desktop and notebook CPUs and APUs), **Gaming** (Radeon discrete GPUs and semi-custom console SoCs), and **Embedded** (Xilinx adaptive SoCs and FPGAs, plus embedded x86 processors). AMD presents Client and Gaming together as "Client and Gaming" for operating income.

### Where AMD operates

AMD is headquartered in Santa Clara, California. It runs major design centers in Austin (Texas), Markham (Ontario, from ATI), Hyderabad and Bangalore (India), Shanghai (China), Cork and Dublin (Ireland, largely from Xilinx), Helsinki (Finland, from Silo AI) and San Jose (California, from Xilinx). AMD bills a large share of revenue to customers in Asia, because ODMs, OEM manufacturing sites and console assemblers are concentrated in Taiwan and China. Billing location does not reflect end demand, which is weighted toward US hyperscale cloud providers and global PC buyers.

## 2. COMPANY HISTORY

### Founding (1969–1981)

AMD was incorporated on 1969-05-01 in Sunnyvale, California, by Jerry Sanders III, a flamboyant former Fairchild Semiconductor sales executive, and seven Fairchild colleagues. The company started as a second-source supplier, making logic chips that other companies designed. Sanders paired a strong sales culture with a pledge to put "people first." AMD went public in 1972, built a reputation for quality (selling parts to military-grade MIL-STD-883 specifications at no premium), and moved into memory and microprocessors in the 1970s.

### The Intel relationship and the x86 license (1982–1995)

In 1982 IBM required a second source for the Intel 8088 used in the original IBM PC, and AMD signed a technology-exchange agreement with Intel. This made AMD a licensed x86 manufacturer, the foundation of the business for the next four decades. When Intel tried to end the arrangement in the mid-1980s, years of arbitration and litigation followed, and AMD's x86 rights were largely upheld in the mid-1990s. AMD launched its own Am386 in 1991 and Am486 in 1993, and established itself as the main alternative to Intel in PCs.

### Building its own architectures (1996–2005)

AMD bought NexGen in 1996 to strengthen in-house CPU design. The K6 (1997) and especially the Athlon (1999), the first x86 processor to reach 1 GHz in 2000, made AMD a performance leader for a time. The defining move of the era was AMD64, the 64-bit extension of x86, which shipped in the Opteron server processor in 2003. Intel eventually adopted the same instruction set, making AMD the author of the x86-64 standard that still underpins PC and server computing. Opteron took meaningful server share by 2005–2006, and in 2005 AMD sued Intel for anticompetitive conduct. The case settled in 2009 with Intel paying AMD USD 1.25 billion and agreeing to a cross-license.

### Overreach and near-collapse (2006–2014)

In 2006 AMD acquired ATI Technologies for about USD 5.4 billion, gaining graphics and chipset capability but taking on heavy debt and large goodwill write-downs. At the same time Intel's Core architecture (2006) recovered the performance lead, and AMD's Barcelona (2007) and Bulldozer (2011) architectures disappointed. To fund itself, AMD spun off its fabs as GlobalFoundries in 2009 (with Abu Dhabi's Mubadala/ATIC) and became fabless, tied to GlobalFoundries by a wafer supply agreement that was renegotiated several times. Through 2012–2015 AMD posted repeated losses, cut staff, sold and leased back its Austin campus, and saw its stock fall below USD 2 in 2015–2016. The semi-custom wins for both the PlayStation 4 and Xbox One (2013) provided a crucial floor of revenue.

### The Lisa Su turnaround (2014–2021)

Lisa Su became CEO on 2014-10-08 and focused AMD on high-performance computing, a long-term roadmap and execution. Key steps:
- **2016:** A joint venture licensing server IP in China (THATIC) raised cash. It was later restricted after the US added related entities to the Entity List in 2019.
- **2017:** The **Zen** architecture launched in **Ryzen** desktop CPUs and **EPYC** "Naples" server CPUs. Zen improved instructions per clock by about 52% over the prior design, and the multi-die (chiplet) approach made high-core-count parts economical.
- **2018–2019:** AMD moved leading-edge production to TSMC after GlobalFoundries abandoned 7 nm. EPYC "Rome" (2019, 7 nm, up to 64 cores) took performance leadership in servers, and server share began a multi-year climb.
- **2020:** Zen 3 took single-thread PC leadership. AMD announced the all-stock acquisition of **Xilinx**.
- **2021:** AMD joined the S&P 500's largest constituents by market value and posted record revenue of about USD 16.4 billion (model knowledge).

### Platform expansion: Xilinx, Pensando and the AI pivot (2022–2024)

- **2022-02-14:** The Xilinx acquisition closed (valued at about USD 49 billion at closing), adding FPGAs, adaptive SoCs and a large embedded, industrial, automotive and communications franchise.
- **2022-05:** AMD acquired **Pensando** (about USD 1.9 billion) for DPUs and programmable networking.
- **2022–2023:** The PC and gaming downturn cut FY2023 revenue to USD 22,680 million (-3.9% YoY, model knowledge), while EPYC "Genoa" (Zen 4) continued server share gains.
- **2023-12-06:** AMD launched the **Instinct MI300X** GPU and **MI300A** APU, its first AI accelerators competitive at scale, with Microsoft, Meta, Oracle and later OpenAI as customers. The MI300A powers El Capitan, which became the world's fastest supercomputer.
- **2024:** Instinct revenue exceeded USD 5 billion in its first full year (management commentary, model knowledge). AMD acquired Silo AI (Finland, about USD 665 million) and in August 2024 announced the acquisition of **ZT Systems** (about USD 4.9 billion) to gain rack-scale system design talent. FY2024 revenue reached USD 25,785 million (+13.7%).

### Rack-scale AI and the hyperscale partnerships (2025–2026)

- **2025-03:** The ZT Systems acquisition closed. AMD kept the roughly 1,000-person design and customer-enablement team and agreed to sell ZT's US data-center infrastructure manufacturing business to Sanmina (closed in late 2025). This explains the "discontinued operations" lines in FY2025 cash flows.
- **2025-04 to 2025-08:** US export controls required licenses for MI308 shipments to China. AMD booked about USD 800 million of inventory and related charges in Q2 FY2025 and about USD 440 million net for FY2025, partly reversed later when some MI308 shipments to China were licensed (about USD 390 million of MI308 revenue in Q4 FY2025).
- **2025-06:** AMD launched the Instinct **MI350/MI355X** series (CDNA 4) and previewed the MI400 series and the Helios rack.
- **2025-10-06:** AMD and **OpenAI** announced a strategic partnership to deploy **6 GW** of AMD GPUs, starting with 1 GW of MI450 in the second half of 2026. AMD issued OpenAI a warrant for up to 160 million AMD shares, vesting in tranches tied to deployment milestones and AMD share-price targets.
- **2025-11-11:** At its Financial Analyst Day, AMD framed a "USD 1 trillion compute market" and set its multi-year targets.
- **FY2025:** Record revenue of USD 34,639 million (+34.3%) and FCF of USD 5,519 million.
- **2026 (CES):** AMD showed Helios, the MI440X, Ryzen AI 400 and the Ryzen 7 9850X3D. HPE adopted Helios, and a Cisco/HUMAIN joint venture targeted 1 GW of AI infrastructure by 2030.
- **H1 2026:** AMD launched the Instinct MI400 series (MI455X for AI, MI430X for HPC/sovereign AI), 6th Gen EPYC "Venice" (Zen 6, 2 nm), ROCm.ai and the MI350P. It signed an agreement with **Anthropic** for up to 2 GW of MI450-based Helios racks, expanded Azure deployments, began an inference collaboration with Cerebras, and acquired MEXT (memory optimization). Q2 FY2026 revenue was a record USD 11,536 million (+50.1%). Tim Ryan (former PwC US chair) joined the board.

## 3. MANAGEMENT TEAM

### Dr. Lisa T. Su, Chair, President and Chief Executive Officer

Lisa Su has been AMD's President and CEO since October 2014 and chair of the board since February 2022, about 12 years at the top and 14 years with the company. She joined AMD in January 2012 as Senior Vice President and General Manager of Global Business Units. There she ran the product groups and championed the semi-custom strategy that won both the PlayStation 4 and Xbox One, giving AMD stable revenue during its weakest years. She became Chief Operating Officer in July 2014 and CEO three months later.

Before AMD, Su spent about 25 years in semiconductors at three companies. At Freescale Semiconductor (2007–2011) she was Chief Technology Officer and later Senior Vice President and General Manager of the Networking and Multimedia group. At IBM (1995–2007) she held senior engineering and management roles, including Vice President of Semiconductor Research and Development. She led work on copper interconnects, a key change in chip manufacturing, and on the Cell processor used in the PlayStation 3. She began her career at Texas Instruments. She holds bachelor's, master's and doctoral degrees in electrical engineering from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, where her PhD work focused on silicon-on-insulator devices.

Her record at AMD is one of the most cited turnarounds in technology. When she took over, AMD had annual revenue of about USD 5.5 billion, heavy debt, negative equity in parts of 2015–2016 and a share price that fell below USD 2. Her strategy rested on three decisions: commit to a multi-generation CPU roadmap (Zen) and execute it on schedule, move to TSMC and chiplets to get leading-edge performance at manageable cost, and reinvest in data-center products where AMD had almost no share. Under her, revenue has grown more than sixfold to USD 34,639 million in FY2025, and the company has made three transformative acquisitions (Xilinx, Pensando, ZT Systems). More recently she has positioned AMD as the main merchant alternative to Nvidia in AI accelerators, personally anchoring the OpenAI and Anthropic agreements. Her recognitions include TIME's 2024 CEO of the Year and election to the National Academy of Engineering. She also sits on the board of Cisco Systems.

**Assessment:** Su is AMD's single most important asset and also a key-person risk. Her credibility with hyperscale customers, TSMC and investors is closely tied to the company's valuation. AMD has not publicly named a successor.

### Jean Hu, Executive Vice President, Chief Financial Officer and Treasurer

Jean Hu became AMD's CFO in January 2023, succeeding Devinder Kumar. She is responsible for finance, treasury, tax, investor relations, internal audit and IT, and she has about four years with the company. She took the role during the 2023 PC and gaming inventory correction and the early MI300 investment ramp. Since then she has handled the integration accounting for ZT Systems, the Sanmina divestiture, the MI308 export-control charges and the structuring of the OpenAI warrant. All of these create heavy GAAP-to-non-GAAP reconciliation work and require clear messaging to investors.

Hu has spent more than 25 years in finance in semiconductors and technology. Immediately before AMD she was CFO of **Marvell Technology** (2016–2022). There she helped Marvell's transformation into a data-infrastructure semiconductor company through the acquisitions of Cavium (2018), Aquantia, Inphi (2021) and Innovium, and through the divestiture of non-core businesses. Before Marvell she was CFO of **QLogic** (2015–2016), where she led its sale to Cavium, and CFO of **Conexant Systems**, where she held several finance and strategy roles over about a decade, including running the company through restructuring. Earlier she held roles at Clarent and at Lehman Brothers, and she began her career in strategy consulting. She holds a PhD in economics and a master's degree in economics from Claremont Graduate University and a bachelor's degree from Tsinghua University (verify degree details against the proxy).

**Assessment:** Hu brings a strong M&A and integration record and credibility with sell-side and buy-side investors. Her guidance under AMD's "beat and raise" cadence has generally been conservative. The key tests ahead are working-capital discipline (inventories were USD 8,468 million at 2026-06-27, high relative to quarterly cost of sales) and funding the AI ramp without hurting free cash flow.

### Mark D. Papermaster, Executive Vice President and Chief Technology Officer

Mark Papermaster has been AMD's CTO since October 2011 and also leads Technology and Engineering, which covers corporate technology strategy, IP, silicon design methodology, product security and the software and system architecture shared across AMD's product lines. He is one of the company's longest-serving leaders, with about 15 years at AMD.

Papermaster has spent nearly 40 years in microprocessor and system engineering. He spent about 26 years at **IBM** (from 1982), where he led development of the POWER and PowerPC processors and moved into senior roles in IBM's server and microelectronics divisions, including Vice President of Microprocessor Development. In 2008 he joined **Apple** as Senior Vice President of Devices Hardware Engineering, responsible for iPhone and iPod hardware. His move from IBM led to well-known non-compete litigation that was later settled. He left Apple in 2010 and joined **Cisco** as Vice President of the Silicon Engineering Group before joining AMD. He holds a bachelor's degree in electrical engineering from the University of Texas at Austin and a master's degree in electrical engineering from the University of Vermont.

His central achievement at AMD is the engineering reset that produced Zen. He rebuilt AMD's design processes, backed the modular "Infinity Fabric" and chiplet architecture, and oversaw the shift to TSMC process nodes. Chiplets allow AMD to mix process nodes, raise yields on large designs and reuse silicon across client, server and accelerator products. The same idea underpins MI300 and MI400, which stack compute dies on I/O dies using advanced packaging. He also champions the open-standards approach (UALink, Ultra Ethernet, open ROCm) that AMD uses to counter Nvidia's proprietary NVLink/CUDA platform.

**Assessment:** Papermaster provides architectural continuity. Given his tenure and age (64 per MarketScreener), succession in the CTO role is a medium-term consideration. Vamsi Boppana (SVP, AI) and other senior technical leaders provide depth.

### Forrest E. Norrod, Executive Vice President and General Manager, Data Center Solutions Business Group

Forrest Norrod has led AMD's data-center business since joining in October 2014, about 12 years. His remit has grown from server CPUs to the full data-center portfolio: EPYC, Instinct accelerators, Pensando DPUs and networking, data-center FPGAs and, since the ZT Systems acquisition, rack-scale systems. The business he runs generated USD 16,635 million in FY2025 (48.0% of revenue) and USD 6,718 million in Q2 FY2026 alone.

Norrod has more than 35 years of experience in computing hardware. Before AMD he spent about 14 years at **Dell** (2000–2014). He finished there as Vice President and General Manager of Dell's server business, responsible for the PowerEdge line, and he founded and ran Dell's **Data Center Solutions** group, which built custom servers for the earliest hyperscale and cloud customers. That experience is directly relevant to AMD's work with hyperscalers today. Before Dell he held engineering and management roles at **Cyrix**, a fellow x86 alternative to Intel in the 1990s, and at **Hewlett-Packard**. He holds bachelor's and master's degrees in electrical engineering from Virginia Tech (per company and public biographies; verify against the proxy).

When Norrod arrived, AMD's server share had fallen to low single digits after the Bulldozer-era Opteron parts. He rebuilt hyperscaler and OEM relationships, ran the EPYC launches from Naples (2017) through Venice (2026), and committed to on-time, generation-by-generation roadmaps, which restored customer confidence. By AMD's own figures, EPYC now holds a large minority of server CPU revenue share, and the 2025 Analyst Day targeted more than 50% share. He has also driven the Instinct roadmap from MI250 to MI300 and now MI400/Helios.

**Assessment:** Norrod has a strong record of customer trust in the most valuable part of the franchise. A third-party profile updated 2026-08-03 still lists his title, but this session did not confirm his status against the FY2026 proxy statement.

### Board composition and governance

- **Board:** eight directors per MarketScreener, with Lisa Su as Chair and the rest independent. Independent directors include Nora Denzel (Lead Independent Director; chair of Governance and Nominating), John Marren (former TPG technology partner; Audit and Finance), Michael Gregoire (former CA Technologies CEO), Abhijit Talwalkar (former LSI CEO), Elizabeth Vanderslice (former Wells Fargo executive), Joseph (KC) McClure (former Accenture CFO; Audit chair) and Timothy Ryan (former PwC US Chair, added 2026). AMD combines the chair and CEO roles, balanced by a lead independent director.
- **Committees:** Audit and Finance; Compensation and Leadership Resources; Nominating and Corporate Governance; Innovation and Technology. The committee-chair dates on MarketScreener are inconsistent and should be checked against the FY2026 proxy (DEF 14A, filed 2026, accession 0001193125-26-129057).
- **Insider ownership:** below 1% of shares outstanding (model knowledge). Lisa Su is the largest individual holder. Institutional ownership is high, led by index funds (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street).
- **Compensation:** CEO pay is heavily equity-weighted, with performance share units tied to relative total shareholder return and multi-year financial goals. A large share of total pay is at risk. Say-on-pay support has historically been strong.
- **Dilution:** stock-based compensation was USD 1,638 million in FY2025 (4.7% of revenue) and USD 990 million in H1 FY2026. Along with the OpenAI warrant (up to about 9.8% of current shares if fully vested), this is a governance item to watch. AMD runs buybacks partly to offset SBC.

### Management track record

AMD's leadership team is long-tenured and unusually stable. Su, Papermaster and Norrod have worked together for about 12 years. The team has hit major roadmap milestones (Zen 1 to Zen 6, MI300 to MI400) largely on time. It has integrated Xilinx with limited disruption, although Embedded revenue fell 3% in FY2025 after a multi-year inventory correction. It has also built a credible data-center AI business from almost nothing in about three years. The main open questions are rack-scale execution (supply chain, networking and system reliability at multi-gigawatt scale) and leadership succession.

## 4. PRODUCTS & SERVICES

### Data Center (48.0% of FY2025 revenue; 58.2% of Q2 FY2026)

**EPYC server CPUs.** Five generations shipped: Naples, Rome, Milan, Genoa/Bergamo/Siena, and Turin (5th Gen, Zen 5/5c, 2024), followed by the 6th Gen "Venice" (Zen 6, TSMC 2 nm), launched in H1 2026. EPYC differentiates on high core counts (up to 192 cores in Turin dense parts and more in Venice), performance per watt, memory bandwidth, and total-cost-of-ownership gains when replacing older Intel Xeon fleets. Customers include every major hyperscaler (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud, Oracle, Meta, Alibaba, Tencent), the main OEMs (Dell, HPE, Lenovo, Supermicro, Cisco) and enterprises. CPU demand is rising again because agentic AI workloads run orchestration, retrieval and tool-calling on general-purpose cores.

**Instinct GPU accelerators.**
- *MI300X/MI300A (CDNA 3, 2023):* 192 GB of HBM3. Used for inference at Microsoft and Meta, and in the El Capitan supercomputer.
- *MI325X (2024) and MI350/MI355X (CDNA 4, mid-2025):* up to 288 GB of HBM3E and FP4/FP6 support, aimed at large-model inference with leading memory capacity.
- *MI400 series (2026):* MI455X (AI training and inference) and MI430X (HPC and sovereign AI with strong FP64). These are the first AMD GPUs designed from the start for rack scale, using HBM4. The MI450 family powers Helios.
- *MI440X and MI350P:* variants for 8-GPU enterprise servers and PCIe form factors.

**Helios rack-scale platform.** A double-wide, 72-GPU rack (MI450/MI455X) that combines Venice EPYC CPUs, Pensando "Vulcano" AI NICs, UALink-over-Ethernet scale-up and Ultra Ethernet scale-out networking, built on the Open Compute Project Open Rack Wide standard. This is AMD's answer to Nvidia's GB200/GB300 NVL72 and Vera Rubin systems. Named deployers include OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle and HPE. The ZT Systems team designs the rack, while manufacturing is outsourced (Sanmina and others).

**Pensando DPUs and AI NICs.** Salina DPUs for front-end networking and storage offload, and Pollara/Vulcano AI NICs, which are among the first to comply with the Ultra Ethernet Consortium specification.

**ROCm software.** AMD's open-source GPU programming stack, with day-zero support in PyTorch, JAX, vLLM, SGLang and Triton. ROCm.ai (2026) packages enterprise-grade inference and training tooling. Software maturity is still AMD's largest competitive gap against CUDA, but it has narrowed meaningfully since 2024.

### Client (30.7% of FY2025 revenue)

The **Ryzen** desktop and notebook families: Ryzen 9000 (Zen 5) desktops, including X3D parts with 3D V-Cache that are the best-selling enthusiast gaming CPUs; the Ryzen AI 300/400 series notebooks with NPUs meeting Copilot+ PC requirements; "Ryzen AI Max/Halo" large-APU systems for local AI workstations; and Ryzen PRO for commercial PCs (now including Ryzen PRO 9000 with 3D V-Cache). Desktop socket AM5 support has been extended through 2029, which reinforces platform loyalty. Client revenue was USD 10,640 million in FY2025 and USD 3,062 million in Q2 FY2026 (+23% YoY), reflecting share gains and higher average selling prices.

### Gaming (11.3% of FY2025 revenue)

- **Semi-custom SoCs:** Sony PlayStation 5/PS5 Pro and Microsoft Xbox Series X|S, plus Valve's Steam Deck and other handhelds. This revenue follows the console cycle. Gaming revenue fell 31% YoY in Q2 FY2026 to USD 779 million because the console generation is late in its life. Both Sony and Microsoft have publicly confirmed that their next-generation platforms will continue with AMD.
- **Radeon discrete GPUs:** the RX 9000 series (RDNA 4, 2025), positioned in the mainstream and upper-mainstream (RX 9070/9070 XT/9070 GRE) rather than at the very top end. Radeon PRO serves workstations.

### Embedded (10.0% of FY2025 revenue)

The former Xilinx portfolio, including Versal adaptive SoCs (AI Core, AI Edge, Premium Gen 2 with memory on package, RF), Zynq UltraScale+ MPSoCs, Kintex/Virtex/Artix/Spartan FPGAs, Alveo accelerator cards and Kria system-on-modules, together with embedded EPYC and Ryzen (including the new Ryzen AI Embedded X100). End markets are aerospace and defense, industrial and robotics, test and measurement, automotive (ADAS, in-cabin), communications (5G radio, wired), medical imaging, broadcast and emulation/prototyping. Embedded revenue fell 3% in FY2025 to USD 3,454 million at the end of a long inventory correction, then recovered to USD 977 million in Q2 FY2026 (+19% YoY) with an operating margin of 39.5%. It is the highest-margin segment.

### Pricing and deal sizes

- Instinct: estimated tens of thousands of USD per GPU (not disclosed). A 1 GW Helios deployment implies tens of billions of USD of AMD revenue over its life (sell-side estimates, not company figures). The OpenAI 6 GW agreement has been widely described as worth "tens of billions" of USD over several years.

## 6. INDUSTRY OVERVIEW

### Definition and scope

AMD competes in the semiconductor industry (NAICS 334413, Semiconductor and Related Device Manufacturing; SIC 3674), specifically in high-performance logic: data-center CPUs, AI accelerators (merchant GPUs and custom ASICs), AI networking, PC processors, discrete and console graphics, and programmable logic (FPGAs and adaptive SoCs). Adjacent industries include foundry and advanced packaging, high-bandwidth memory, server ODM manufacturing and AI infrastructure (power, cooling, data-center construction).

### Market size and structure

- **Total semiconductors:** about USD 700–800 billion in 2025 by WSTS/industry estimates (model knowledge, approximate), rising toward about USD 1 trillion by 2030. Logic and memory are the largest categories, and AI data-center silicon is now the main growth engine.
- **Data-center AI accelerators:** the fastest-growing segment. AMD sized the AI accelerator TAM at about USD 500 billion by 2028 at its 2024–2025 events. At its 2025 Analyst Day it framed a total data-center TAM (CPUs, GPUs, ASICs, networking) of about USD 1 trillion by 2030. Nvidia holds a dominant share (well above 80% of merchant GPU revenue by most estimates). Custom ASICs (Google TPU, AWS Trainium, Meta MTIA, Microsoft Maia, OpenAI/Broadcom) are a growing share.
- **Server CPUs:** roughly USD 30–40 billion per year (model knowledge), a near-duopoly of Intel and AMD, with Arm-based custom CPUs (AWS Graviton, Google Axion, Microsoft Cobalt, Nvidia Grace/Vera) gaining share.
- **PC processors:** about USD 35–45 billion per year (approximate). Intel leads by units and AMD is a strong number two. Arm entrants (Qualcomm Snapdragon X, Apple M-series, MediaTek/Nvidia) are emerging.
- **FPGAs and adaptive SoCs:** about USD 10–12 billion. AMD (Xilinx) and Altera (spun out of Intel, majority-owned by Silver Lake since 2025) hold most of the market, with Lattice and Microchip at the low end.
- **Gaming GPUs and consoles:** discrete PC graphics led by Nvidia (roughly 85–90% unit share, approximate). AMD supplies all current Sony and Microsoft consoles.

### Growth rates

- AI data-center capex by the top US hyperscalers rose to several hundred billion USD per year in 2025–2026 (company disclosures, model knowledge), with continued growth expected into 2027.
- AMD's own Data Center revenue grew 32% in FY2025 and 107% YoY in Q2 FY2026.
- The PC market is growing modestly (low single digits) as Windows 10 end-of-support (October 2025) and AI-PC refresh cycles play out.
- Embedded is in a cyclical recovery after a two-year inventory correction.

### Key trends and drivers

1. **Shift from training to inference.** Inference workloads are expected to exceed training in compute demand. This favors memory-rich GPUs (AMD's design focus) and cost per token, and it opens the market to alternatives to Nvidia.
2. **Rack-scale systems.** The unit of competition has moved from the chip to the 72-GPU rack with scale-up networking. Vendors without system and networking capability fall behind, which is why AMD bought ZT Systems.
3. **Open versus proprietary ecosystems.** UALink, Ultra Ethernet and open-source software stacks (PyTorch, Triton) reduce the lock-in from CUDA and NVLink.
4. **Custom silicon.** Hyperscalers increasingly design their own accelerators with Broadcom, Marvell, MediaTek and Alchip, which competes with merchant GPUs.
5. **Agentic AI raising CPU demand.** Agents use general-purpose CPUs for orchestration, which supports a server CPU refresh.
6. **Power as the binding constraint.** Deals are now sized in gigawatts. Performance per watt and time to power determine wins.
7. **Supply chain bottlenecks.** TSMC CoWoS capacity, HBM supply and the advanced nodes (N3/N2) constrain the whole industry.

### Regulatory environment

- **US export controls** (BIS rules from 2022, 2023, 2025 and later) restrict sales of advanced AI chips to China and other destinations, require licenses for products such as the MI308, and have caused inventory write-downs. Revenue-sharing arrangements on licensed China sales have been reported (model knowledge).
- **Tariffs and Section 232 semiconductor investigations** create cost and pricing uncertainty. TSMC Arizona production partly mitigates this.
- **CHIPS Act** incentives favor US manufacturing, mostly benefiting foundries rather than fabless AMD.
- **Antitrust:** AMD's scale is now large enough that future large acquisitions would face scrutiny in several jurisdictions (EU, China SAMR, UK CMA).

### Industry dynamics (Porter view)

- **Barriers to entry:** very high (multi-billion-dollar design costs at 2 nm, an x86 license duopoly, the software ecosystem, and supply-chain access).
- **Supplier power:** high. TSMC and the HBM makers are concentrated suppliers with pricing power.
- **Buyer power:** high and rising. Hyperscalers and AI labs are few, sophisticated, and able to build their own silicon.
- **Substitutes:** custom ASICs and Arm CPUs.
- **Rivalry:** intense. Nvidia releases on an annual cadence, and Intel is restructuring aggressively.

## 7. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

### 1. Nvidia (NVDA), the main AI competitor

Nvidia is the dominant supplier of AI accelerators. Its revenue run rate far exceeds AMD's total revenue (more than USD 200 billion annualized by 2026, model knowledge, approximate), and its data-center gross margins are about 70–75%. Its strengths are CUDA and a software ecosystem nearly two decades old, NVLink/NVSwitch scale-up, Spectrum-X and InfiniBand networking, full-stack rack systems (GB300 NVL72, Vera Rubin NVL144), an annual product cadence and priority access to CoWoS and HBM supply. **AMD's position:** competitive or better on memory capacity per GPU and on price and TCO for inference, behind on software maturity, networking scale and market share. AMD's AI GPU share is in the single digits to low teens. Each 1 point of share in a market of several hundred billion USD is worth billions of USD of revenue to AMD.

### 2. Intel (INTC), the main CPU competitor

Intel remains the largest x86 vendor by units in both servers and PCs, with annual revenue of about USD 50+ billion (model knowledge, approximate). It is in a multi-year restructuring under CEO Lip-Bu Tan, with US government and Nvidia equity investments in 2025 (model knowledge), and is pursuing the 18A process node, Xeon 6/Clearwater Forest servers and Panther Lake PCs. **AMD's position:** AMD has taken server CPU revenue share from low single digits in 2017 to roughly 40% (Mercury Research and management commentary, approximate). It also leads in enthusiast desktop and is gaining in commercial notebooks. Intel's discounting and any 18A process success are risks to AMD's further gains.

### 3. Broadcom (AVGO), custom AI ASICs and networking

Broadcom co-designs custom accelerators (XPUs) with Google (TPU), Meta, ByteDance, OpenAI and others, and leads Ethernet switch silicon (Tomahawk, Jericho). **AMD's position:** custom ASICs compete with Instinct for hyperscaler budgets. In networking, AMD (Pensando) is a smaller player. Broadcom is also a partner in the Ultra Ethernet ecosystem.

### 4. Marvell Technology (MRVL), custom silicon and data infrastructure

Marvell is a custom AI ASIC partner (AWS Trainium, Microsoft Maia) and supplies electro-optics and DPUs. It competes indirectly with AMD on custom accelerators and directly with Pensando on DPUs.

### 5. Hyperscaler in-house silicon (Google TPU, AWS Trainium/Graviton, Microsoft Maia/Cobalt, Meta MTIA)

These chips substitute for both Instinct (AI accelerators) and EPYC (Arm CPUs). Graviton has reached a large share of new AWS CPU deployments. **AMD's position:** AMD argues that merchant silicon offers a faster roadmap and a multi-cloud software ecosystem, and its open-standards approach makes it the "second source" partner hyperscalers prefer when building alongside their own chips.

### 6. Qualcomm (QCOM) and Arm-based PC entrants (also Apple, MediaTek/Nvidia)

Snapdragon X Elite/X2 Windows PCs, Apple's M-series Macs, and Arm PC chips from Nvidia and MediaTek compete with Ryzen on battery life and NPU performance. Qualcomm has also announced data-center AI inference products (AI200/AI250). **AMD's position:** x86 compatibility and gaming performance protect the core, but Arm share in premium notebooks is a structural risk.

### 7. Altera (Silver Lake / Intel) and Lattice Semiconductor (LSCC), FPGAs

Altera is the main FPGA rival to AMD's Xilinx franchise. Lattice leads in low-power, small FPGAs. **AMD's position:** a clear number one in FPGAs and adaptive SoCs (the Analyst Day target is more than 70% share of adaptive computing), with Versal AI Edge and Premium Gen 2 widening the lead.

### 8. AI accelerator start-ups (Cerebras, Groq, SambaNova, Tenstorrent, Etched, d-Matrix)

Specialized inference architectures, each with niche traction. Some, such as Cerebras, are now AMD collaborators. They are a small but growing competitive factor in inference.

### Positioning framework

| Dimension | AMD | Nvidia | Intel | Custom ASIC (Broadcom/Marvell partners) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| AI accelerator performance | High (memory-rich) | Highest | Low (Gaudi, Jaguar Shores pending) | High for target workloads |
| Software ecosystem | Improving (ROCm) | Best-in-class (CUDA) | Limited | Proprietary per hyperscaler |
| Rack-scale systems | New (Helios, 2026) | Mature (NVL72) | Limited | Customer-designed |
| Server CPU | Leader in performance | Grace/Vera (Arm) | Incumbent by units | Graviton/Axion/Cobalt (Arm) |
| PC CPU | Strong number two | Entering (Arm) | Number one by units | n/a |
| FPGA/adaptive | Number one | n/a | Altera (divested) | n/a |
| Supply access | TSMC N2/N3, CoWoS | Priority TSMC, CoWoS | Own fabs plus TSMC | TSMC |

### Competitive advantages

1. **The broadest high-performance compute portfolio** among merchant vendors (CPU + GPU + DPU + FPGA + racks), which allows full-system TCO selling.
2. **Chiplet and advanced packaging leadership.** Proven over 9 years, this enables fast product derivatives and favorable yields.
3. **Memory capacity leadership** on Instinct, which suits inference.
4. **Open-ecosystem positioning**, which appeals to customers seeking a second source and leverage over Nvidia.
5. **Anchor commitments** from OpenAI (6 GW) and Anthropic (up to 2 GW) that reduce demand risk for MI450.
6. **A track record of on-time execution** under a stable leadership team.

### Competitive vulnerabilities

1. **Software (ROCm) maturity** and developer mindshare compared with CUDA.
2. **Scale-up networking.** UALink adoption is still early compared with mature NVLink.
3. **Scale disadvantage** in R&D spending and supply allocation compared with Nvidia.
4. **Dependence on a few very large AI customers** and on equity-linked incentives (the OpenAI warrant).
5. **Gaming and console cyclicality**, and Arm encroachment in PCs and cloud CPUs.

### Market share summary (approximate, model knowledge)

- Server CPU revenue share: about 40% (unit share in the 30%s). Target: more than 50%.
- Client CPU revenue share: about 25–30%. Target: more than 40%.
- AI accelerator share: high single digits to low double digits of merchant GPU revenue.
- FPGA share: about 50–60%. Adaptive computing target: more than 70%.
- Console SoC share: about 100% of current-generation Sony and Microsoft consoles.

## 9. RISK ASSESSMENT

### Company-specific risks

**1. Execution risk on MI450/Helios ramp (High severity, Medium likelihood).** The H2 2026 ramp is AMD's first rack-scale, gigawatt-class delivery. It combines new HBM4 memory, a new 2 nm CPU, new UALink/Ultra Ethernet networking and liquid-cooled racks built by contract manufacturers. Delays or reliability issues would hit the "accelerate in H2 2026" guidance and the OpenAI warrant milestones, and could cost AMD credibility as Nvidia's alternative. *Mitigants:* the ZT Systems design team, Sanmina's manufacturing scale, and an extended customer co-validation period.

**2. Customer concentration (High severity, Medium likelihood).** A small group (OpenAI, Meta, Microsoft, Oracle, Anthropic, Sony) will drive a disproportionate share of FY2026–FY2028 revenue. A pause by any one, for example because of funding constraints at an AI lab or a shift toward in-house ASICs, could remove several billion USD of revenue. OpenAI's commitment depends partly on its own fundraising and compute economics. *Mitigants:* a diversified list of AI customers and a broad non-AI business (Client, Embedded, EPYC).

**3. Key-person dependency (Medium severity, Low likelihood).** Lisa Su is closely identified with AMD's strategy and customer relationships, and no successor has been publicly identified. Several senior leaders (Papermaster, Norrod) have more than 12 years in their roles. *Mitigants:* a deep bench (Hu, Boppana, Huynh, Guido) and a strong board.

**4. Software ecosystem gap (Medium severity, Medium likelihood).** ROCm still trails CUDA in breadth, tooling and developer familiarity. If hyperscaler and AI-lab engineers need significant porting work, AMD's TCO advantage narrows. *Mitigants:* PyTorch/Triton abstraction layers, day-zero model support and OpenAI's co-development.

**5. Shareholder dilution and SBC (Medium severity, High likelihood).** SBC was USD 1,638 million in FY2025 (4.7% of revenue), and the OpenAI warrant could add up to 160 million shares (about 9.8% of the 2026-01-30 count) if all tranches vest. Vesting requires share-price milestones and would occur alongside value creation, but it dilutes per-share upside. *Mitigants:* buybacks, and vesting tied to stock-price targets.

**6. Integration and M&A risk (Low–Medium severity).** Goodwill of USD 25,470 million (Xilinx, ZT Systems) carries impairment risk if Embedded or systems businesses underperform. Further tuck-ins (Silo AI, MEXT, Enosemi, Brium, Untether AI team) add integration complexity.

### Industry and market risks

**7. Competitive intensity from Nvidia and custom ASICs (High severity, High likelihood).** Nvidia's annual cadence (Blackwell Ultra, then Rubin, then Rubin Ultra) and its control of supply allocation may limit AMD's share gains. Growth in hyperscaler custom ASICs (TPU, Trainium) could shrink the merchant GPU market. Price competition could compress AMD's AI GPU gross margins below the corporate average. *Mitigants:* memory-capacity and TCO advantages, and buyers wanting a second source.

**8. Export controls and China (Medium–High severity, High likelihood).** US rules have already cost about USD 800 million in Q2 FY2025 charges and continue to restrict AI GPU sales to China. China (including Hong Kong) is also a significant billing destination for client and embedded products. Further rules, retaliation (for example Chinese bans on US chips in state systems) or tariff changes could hurt revenue and margins.

**9. Supply chain concentration (High severity, Low–Medium likelihood).** Nearly all leading-edge silicon and advanced packaging comes from TSMC in Taiwan, and HBM comes from three suppliers. Capacity shortages (CoWoS, HBM4) or a geopolitical disruption in the Taiwan Strait would severely impair supply. *Mitigants:* TSMC Arizona qualification and multi-sourcing of HBM.

**10. AI capex cyclicality (High severity, Medium likelihood).** AI infrastructure spending is concentrated, partly funded with debt and equity by AI labs and neoclouds, and could pause if model monetization disappoints. The semiconductor industry has historically had sharp inventory corrections, for example the 2022–2023 PC/gaming correction and the 2023–2025 embedded correction.

### Financial risks

**11. Working capital and gross margin dilution (Medium severity, Medium likelihood).** Inventories reached USD 8,468 million at 2026-06-27 as AMD builds for the rack ramp. Rack-level systems carry pass-through content (memory, networking, mechanicals) at lower gross margin. Q2 FY2026 GAAP gross margin was 54% and non-GAAP 56%. A shift toward systems could hold margins below the long-term target. Another export-control write-down is also possible.

**12. Valuation and capital market risk (High severity, Medium likelihood).** At about USD 988.5 billion of market cap (roughly 28.5x FY2025 sales), the shares discount several years of execution. A small miss could drive large drawdowns, as the 52-week range of about USD 188–659 shows (stockevents.app). The balance sheet is strong (net cash of about USD 9.9 billion; total debt of USD 3,226 million), so solvency is not a concern.

### Macroeconomic risks

**13. Economic sensitivity of PCs, gaming and embedded (Medium severity).** About 52% of FY2025 revenue (Client, Gaming, Embedded) is consumer- or industrial-cycle sensitive. A recession would cut PC and console units and delay industrial design-ins.

**14. Interest rates and FX (Low–Medium severity).** Higher rates raise the cost of capital for AI-lab customers and neoclouds that fund large GPU purchases with debt, and they compress valuation multiples. AMD sells mostly in USD, so direct FX exposure is limited, but a strong dollar raises local-currency prices for international PC buyers.

**15. Geopolitical and tariff risk (High severity, Low–Medium likelihood).** US–China tensions, possible Section 232 semiconductor tariffs, and Taiwan risk affect both supply (TSMC) and demand (China billings). Tariffs could raise system costs for US customers buying racks assembled abroad.
