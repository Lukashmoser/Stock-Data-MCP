## 1. COMPANY OVERVIEW

### What AMD does

Advanced Micro Devices is a fabless semiconductor company that designs high-performance compute silicon: server and PC microprocessors (CPUs), data center AI accelerators (GPUs), consumer graphics processors, semi-custom system-on-chips (SoCs) for game consoles, field-programmable gate arrays (FPGAs) and adaptive SoCs, data-processing units and AI networking cards, and, since the 2025 acquisition of ZT Systems, rack-scale system designs. AMD does not own fabs. It designs chips and licenses or builds the surrounding software (most importantly the ROCm GPU software stack). TSMC makes its leading-edge wafers and GlobalFoundries makes most mature-node wafers, and outsourced partners handle assembly, test and packaging.

Put simply, AMD is the only company that sells competitive, high-volume products against Intel in x86 CPUs and against Nvidia in GPUs. That dual position is what defines the investment case. AMD has spent a decade taking CPU share from Intel and is now trying to become the credible second source, and in some workloads the preferred source, for AI accelerators in a market Nvidia dominates.

### How AMD makes money

AMD sells chips, and increasingly full rack-level systems, to original equipment manufacturers (OEMs such as Dell, HPE, Lenovo, Supermicro and HP), to original design manufacturers (ODMs), directly to cloud and AI hyperscalers (Microsoft, Meta, Oracle, OpenAI, Google, AWS and others), to console makers (Sony and Microsoft) under semi-custom contracts, and through distributors to the long tail of PC builders and embedded customers. Revenue is recognized mostly at shipment. The model is purely transactional hardware; there is no material subscription or software revenue. Pricing power comes from performance per dollar and performance per watt at the leading edge.

### Scale and financial trajectory

AMD's revenue went from USD 23,601 million in FY2022 to USD 34,639 million in FY2025. The path was uneven: revenue fell 3.9% in FY2023 during the PC and gaming inventory correction, rose 13.7% in FY2024 as Instinct MI300 ramped, and then rose 34.3% in FY2025 on server CPU share gains, a strong Ryzen cycle and continued AI GPU growth. Growth has accelerated in FY2026. Q1 FY2026 revenue was USD 10,253 million (+37.8% YoY). Q2 FY2026 revenue was a record USD 11,536 million (+50.1% YoY), with Data Center at 58% of company revenue. Management guided Q3 FY2026 to about USD 13,000 million +/- 300 million, roughly 41% YoY growth at the midpoint, with non-GAAP gross margin of about 56%.

GAAP profitability understates underlying earnings power. That is mainly because amortization of the intangibles acquired with Xilinx (2022) and ZT Systems (2025) remains large: intangibles still stood at USD 15,635 million on 2026-06-27. GAAP operating margin was 10.7% in FY2025 (USD 3,694 million), against a non-GAAP operating income of USD 7,768 million (22.4% margin). In Q2 FY2026, GAAP operating income was USD 1,990 million (17.3% margin) and non-GAAP was USD 3,094 million (26.8%). FY2025 GAAP results also absorbed about USD 440 million of net inventory and related charges tied to US export controls on the MI308 GPU for China. Q2 FY2025 was the low point: a GAAP operating loss of USD 134 million and a Data Center segment loss of USD 155 million.

Cash generation has improved sharply. Operating cash flow was USD 1,667 million in FY2023, USD 3,041 million in FY2024 and USD 7,709 million in FY2025 (XBRL total, which includes discontinued operations). Company-defined free cash flow for FY2025 was USD 5,519 million (16% margin), versus USD 2,405 million in FY2024. Stock-based compensation was USD 1,638 million in FY2025 (4.7% of revenue). The balance sheet is conservative. On 2026-06-27 AMD held USD 5,086 million of cash and USD 8,025 million of short-term investments (USD 13,111 million combined) against USD 3,226 million of debt, so net cash was about USD 9,885 million. Total assets were USD 84,464 million, of which goodwill (USD 25,470 million) and intangibles (USD 15,635 million) are about 49%, a legacy of the Xilinx deal.

### Geographic footprint and people

AMD is headquartered in Santa Clara, California, and had about 31,000 employees at FY2025 year-end. Major engineering centers are in Austin, the San Francisco Bay Area, Markham (Canada), Hyderabad and Bengaluru (India), Shanghai, Dublin/Cork (Ireland, ex-Xilinx), Helsinki (ex-Silo AI) and Taiwan (MK). International sales are a significant majority of revenue on a ship-to basis, because most OEMs and ODMs build in Asia. Customer concentration is meaningful but not disclosed above 10% in the FY2025 10-K. The company says that "a small number of customers will continue to account for a significant part of our revenue and receivables."

### Market value

At USD 605.53 per share and 1,632 million shares outstanding, AMD's equity value is about USD 988,513 million. That is roughly 28.5x FY2025 revenue and about 21.4x annualized Q2 FY2026 revenue. The stock has traded between USD 188.22 and USD 658.52 over the past 52 weeks. The valuation implies the market already credits a large part of management's November 2025 long-term targets: revenue CAGR above 35%, non-GAAP operating margin above 35%, and non-GAAP EPS above USD 20 over three to five years.

## 2. COMPANY HISTORY

### Founding (1969-1980s)

AMD was incorporated on 1969-05-01 by W. J. "Jerry" Sanders III and seven colleagues, most of them from Fairchild Semiconductor (MK). The company started in Sunnyvale, California, as a second-source supplier of logic chips, building parts designed by others to higher quality standards. Sanders' sales culture and the "Real men have fabs" ethos defined AMD for three decades. In 1975 AMD entered the memory market and introduced the Am2900 bit-slice family. Its defining early event came in 1982, when IBM required a second source for the Intel 8088/8086 used in the IBM PC and Intel licensed AMD to make x86 processors. The later breakdown of that arrangement led to years of litigation. A 1994-1995 settlement and arbitration confirmed AMD's right to the x86 microcode it had used, and AMD's x86 licence became the company's most important strategic asset (MK).

### Independent x86 design (1990s-2000s)

AMD launched the Am386 in 1991 and the Am486 in 1993. Its first fully internal design, the K5, was late and uncompetitive, so in 1996 AMD bought NexGen, whose design team produced the K6 (MK). The Athlon (K7, 1999) was the first x86 chip to reach 1 GHz commercially (2000). The Opteron server processor and the AMD64 64-bit extension (2003) were AMD's high-water mark of that era: Intel adopted AMD's 64-bit instruction set, and AMD reached roughly 20%-25% of server CPU units by 2006 (MK). In 2006 AMD acquired graphics maker ATI Technologies for about USD 5.4 billion (MK). That deal created today's Radeon and Instinct GPU franchises and later the console semi-custom business, but its timing coincided with Intel's Core architecture comeback and left AMD over-levered.

### Crisis and restructuring (2008-2014)

Under financial pressure, AMD spun off its manufacturing operations in 2009 into GlobalFoundries, a joint venture with Abu Dhabi's Mubadala (MK). That made AMD fabless but bound it to wafer-supply agreements that took years to unwind. In 2009 Intel paid AMD USD 1.25 billion to settle antitrust claims (MK). The Bulldozer CPU architecture (2011) underperformed, and AMD's server share collapsed to low single digits. In 2012 the company bought SeaMicro (later shut down). A turning point came in 2013, when AMD won the semi-custom SoC designs for both the Sony PlayStation 4 and the Microsoft Xbox One. That provided steady cash during the downturn (MK).

### The Lisa Su turnaround (2014-2021)

Lisa Su became CEO on 2014-10-08 (MK). She narrowed the strategy to high-performance computing and committed to a multi-generation CPU roadmap built on the clean-sheet Zen core designed under Jim Keller and Mike Clark. Ryzen desktop CPUs (2017) and the first EPYC server CPUs ("Naples", 2017) restored competitiveness. In 2018 AMD moved its leading-edge production entirely to TSMC after GlobalFoundries abandoned 7nm. The 7nm "Rome" EPYC (2019), which used a chiplet architecture, put AMD ahead of Intel in performance per socket for the first time in over a decade. "Milan" (2021) and "Genoa" (2022) extended the lead. AMD's server CPU revenue share rose from under 2% in 2017 to around 40% by 2025 (Mercury Research, MK). AMD also won the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X|S SoCs (2020).

### Diversification by acquisition (2022-2025)

- **Xilinx (closed 2022-02-14):** an all-stock acquisition valued at about USD 49 billion at closing (MK). It added the FPGA and adaptive SoC leader and created the Embedded segment. It also produced most of AMD's goodwill and intangibles.
- **Pensando (2022):** about USD 1.9 billion (MK). It brought DPUs and the networking talent behind today's Pollara AI NIC and the planned Vulcano NIC.
- **Silo AI (2024):** about USD 665 million (MK). Europe's largest private AI lab, it strengthened model and software expertise.
- **ZT Systems (closed March 2025):** about USD 4.9 billion including contingent consideration (MK). AMD kept the rack-scale system design team and sold ZT's manufacturing business to Sanmina in October 2025, with up to USD 450 million of earn-out receivable through 2028 (USD 348 million recorded on 2026-06-27, per the 10-Q).
- **Smaller deals:** Nod.ai (2023), Mipsology (2023), Brium, Enosemi and Untether AI team (2025), and MEXT (2026, memory optimization).

### AI accelerator pivot and recent developments (2023-2026)

AMD launched the Instinct MI300X/MI300A in December 2023. Data center GPU revenue exceeded USD 5 billion in 2024 (MK). The MI325X followed in late 2024, and the MI350/MI355X series in mid-2025. Major milestones since then:

- **2025-04 to 2025-06:** The US government imposed licence requirements on MI308 exports to China. That led to about USD 800 million of inventory charges in Q2 FY2025, partly reversed later; net FY2025 charges were about USD 440 million.
- **2025-10:** A strategic agreement with OpenAI to deploy 6 gigawatts of AMD GPUs, starting with 1 GW of MI450 in the second half of 2026. AMD issued OpenAI a warrant for up to 160 million AMD shares that vests against deployment and share-price milestones (warrant terms MK).
- **2025-11-11:** At its Financial Analyst Day, AMD set targets of revenue CAGR above 35%, Data Center CAGR above 60%, Data Center AI CAGR above 80%, non-GAAP operating margin above 35% and non-GAAP EPS above USD 20, against a USD 1 trillion compute TAM.
- **2026-01 (CES):** AMD previewed the Helios rack-scale platform and the MI440X, and HPE committed to Helios.
- **2026 H1:** AMD launched the MI400 family (MI455X, MI430X) and 6th Gen EPYC ("Venice"). Helios entered deployment with Anthropic (up to 2 GW of MI450 planned), Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle and others.
- **2026-08-05:** Record Q2 FY2026 revenue (USD 11,536 million) and Data Center revenue (USD 6,718 million, +107% YoY).

## 3. MANAGEMENT TEAM

*Biographical details are from model knowledge (MK) and AMD public materials, and were not re-verified against the 2026 DEF 14A, whose officer section did not render in this session. Titles are as last known; confirm against the 2026 proxy before publication.*

### Dr. Lisa T. Su, Chair, President and Chief Executive Officer

Lisa Su has been AMD's President and CEO since October 2014 and Chair of the Board since February 2022, which makes her one of the longest-tenured CEOs among large-cap semiconductor companies. She joined AMD in January 2012 as Senior Vice President and General Manager of global business units, responsible for driving end-to-end execution of the product portfolio. In mid-2014 she was promoted to Chief Operating Officer, a few months before succeeding Rory Read as CEO.

Before AMD, Su spent more than two decades in semiconductor engineering and management. She started her career at Texas Instruments in 1994. From 1995 to 2007 she was at IBM, where she rose to Vice President of the Semiconductor Research and Development Center. There she led work on copper interconnects and silicon-on-insulator technology and was associated with the Cell processor collaboration with Sony and Toshiba that powered the PlayStation 3. She then served as Chief Technology Officer at Freescale Semiconductor (2007-2011) and later as Senior Vice President and General Manager of Freescale's networking and multimedia group.

Su holds bachelor's, master's and doctoral degrees in electrical engineering from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Her doctoral work focused on silicon-on-insulator devices. She is a member of the US National Academy of Engineering and a Fellow of the IEEE, and she has served on the boards of Cisco Systems and the Semiconductor Industry Association. She has received numerous industry honours, including the Robert N. Noyce Award.

**Track record assessment.** Su's record is the core of the AMD investment case. She inherited a company with a market value of about USD 2-3 billion, a failing CPU architecture, heavy debt and dependence on a captive foundry. Her decisions to bet the company on the Zen roadmap, to move leading-edge production to TSMC, to adopt chiplets early and to execute a consistent annual cadence turned AMD into a company valued near USD 1 trillion. The Xilinx merger, the decision to treat AI GPUs as AMD's top strategic priority from 2023, and the ZT Systems acquisition to gain rack-scale capability show a willingness to make large, forward-looking bets. The main open question is not her execution history. It is whether AMD can turn a strong hardware roadmap into durable AI accelerator share against Nvidia's integrated hardware, networking and software platform. Key-person dependency on Su is high (see Risk Assessment).

### Jean Hu, Executive Vice President, Chief Financial Officer and Treasurer

Jean Hu became AMD's CFO in February 2022, succeeding Devinder Kumar. She oversees finance, accounting, tax, treasury, investor relations and IT. She joined just as the Xilinx acquisition closed, and she has managed the purchase-accounting complexity of that deal, the capital-allocation framework (consistent buybacks, a net-cash balance sheet), the ZT Systems acquisition and the subsequent sale of its manufacturing arm to Sanmina, and the financial structuring of the OpenAI warrant agreement.

Before AMD, Hu was CFO of Marvell Technology from 2016 to 2022. There she helped lead Marvell's transformation from a diversified, under-earning chip company into a data infrastructure specialist, including the acquisitions of Cavium (2018), Aquantia, Avera (GlobalFoundries' ASIC business) and Inphi (2021). Before Marvell she was CFO of QLogic (2013-2016), where she was also interim CEO during a strategic review and helped negotiate its sale to Cavium. Earlier she was CFO of Conexant Systems and held strategy and finance roles at Intel's flash memory business, and she started her career at a technology-focused consultancy and in semiconductor strategy roles (MK).

Hu holds a Ph.D. in economics from Claremont Graduate University and a master's degree in economics (MK). She has served on the board of Lam Research.

**Track record assessment.** Hu brings deep experience in semiconductor M&A integration and data-center-oriented portfolio repositioning, both directly relevant to AMD's current phase. Under her tenure AMD has kept net cash while absorbing two large acquisitions, has raised free cash flow from USD 1,121 million (FY2023, OCF less capex) to USD 5,519 million (FY2025, company definition), and has framed a long-term model with operating margins above 35%. Investors should watch how she balances rising working-capital and supply commitments for rack-scale AI (inventory rose to USD 8,468 million by 2026-06-27), up to USD 5.0 billion of new post-quarter investment commitments disclosed in the Q2 FY2026 10-Q, and USD 4.1 billion of guarantees on partners' data center leases, against the buyback programme.

### Mark Papermaster, Chief Technology Officer and Executive Vice President, Technology and Engineering

Mark Papermaster joined AMD in October 2011 as CTO and Senior Vice President. He was later promoted to Executive Vice President with responsibility for corporate technical direction, product development across CPUs, GPUs and SoCs, system-on-chip methodology, IP, and the company's central engineering organization. He is widely credited, together with Su, with the multi-generation Zen roadmap and AMD's early commitment to chiplet architectures and advanced packaging (2.5D and 3D V-Cache). These are now the foundation of both EPYC and Instinct.

Papermaster spent about 26 years at IBM (1982-2008) in technology and server development roles, including leading development of the POWER microprocessor line and IBM's blade server technology. In 2008 he joined Apple as Senior Vice President of Devices Hardware Engineering, responsible for iPod and iPhone hardware. He left Apple in 2010 and then served as Vice President of the Silicon Engineering Group at Cisco Systems before joining AMD (MK).

He holds a bachelor's degree in electrical engineering from the University of Texas at Austin and a master's degree in electrical engineering from the University of Vermont. He is an IEEE Fellow and has served on industry and academic advisory boards, including the Global Semiconductor Alliance (MK).

**Track record assessment.** Papermaster's tenure includes an unusually reliable cadence of on-schedule architectural launches. Zen through Zen 5 and the 6th Gen EPYC launch in 2026 have all landed broadly on roadmap, which is rare in an industry known for slips. The engineering challenge now shifts to system-level design: rack-scale Helios, Infinity Fabric and UALink-based scale-up, Ultra Ethernet scale-out, and HBM4 integration. Here AMD's experience is shorter than Nvidia's. Succession in the CTO role is a long-term governance consideration given his tenure.

### Forrest Norrod, Executive Vice President and General Manager, Data Center Solutions Business Group

Forrest Norrod has led AMD's data center business since joining in October 2014. He is responsible for EPYC server processors, Instinct accelerators, Pensando networking and, since 2025, the rack-scale systems business built from the ZT Systems design team. Under his leadership AMD's data center segment grew from a negligible base to USD 16,635 million in FY2025 and a USD 26,872 million annualized run rate in Q2 FY2026 (USD 6,718 million x 4).

Before AMD, Norrod was Vice President and General Manager of Dell's server business (2009-2014). There he oversaw Dell's enterprise and hyperscale server lines, including the Data Center Solutions custom server unit that sold directly to large cloud operators. That experience gave him first-hand understanding of how hyperscalers buy compute, which has been central to EPYC adoption at AWS, Microsoft, Google, Meta and Oracle. Earlier in his career he held engineering and management roles at Dell (chief technology officer of the enterprise group), Cyrix, Hewlett-Packard and Linus Technologies (MK).

Norrod holds bachelor's and master's degrees in electrical engineering from Virginia Tech and is named on numerous US patents (MK).

**Track record assessment.** Norrod's server CPU execution is strong. AMD went from almost no server share to a leading position by revenue in under a decade. His second act, AI accelerators and rack-scale systems, is more demanding and is what will drive the stock. The MI400/Helios ramp beginning in the second half of 2026, delivery against the OpenAI 6 GW and Anthropic 2 GW commitments, and the transition from selling chips to selling integrated racks with partners such as Sanmina and Celestica (MK) are the key milestones by which his organization will be judged.

### Board and governance

The 2026 proxy (DEF 14A filed for the 2026-05-13 virtual annual meeting) shows nine directors, falling to eight after the meeting following Jon A. Olson's retirement. Seven of the eight nominees are independent; only Dr. Su is not. Because Su is both Chair and CEO, AMD has a Lead Independent Director (historically John W. Marren, MK). Directors bring semiconductor, technology and finance backgrounds. Tim Ryan was appointed to the board in early 2026 (press release title, Q4 FY2025 results page). Insider ownership is small: directors and officers as a group hold well under 1% of shares outstanding (MK; the 2026 ownership table was not retrievable). The largest holders are index and institutional managers (Vanguard, BlackRock, State Street; MK). Executive pay is heavily weighted to performance-based equity, so management incentives are tied to share price and relative TSR.

## 4. PRODUCTS & SERVICES

### Data Center (48.0% of FY2025 revenue; 58.2% of Q2 FY2026)

**EPYC server CPUs.** AMD's x86 server processors are sold to hyperscalers, enterprises, HPC centres and telecom operators. The generations so far are Rome (Zen 2, 2019), Milan (Zen 3), Genoa/Bergamo/Siena (Zen 4/4c, 2022-2023), Turin (Zen 5/5c, 2024) and 6th Gen EPYC "Venice" (Zen 6 on TSMC 2nm, launched 2026). EPYC's advantages are core count (up to 192 cores in Turin, MK), memory bandwidth, performance per watt and total cost of ownership. Hyperscalers use EPYC both as general-purpose cloud instances and as head-node CPUs in AI servers. Agentic AI is lifting general-purpose CPU demand, a trend AMD highlighted throughout 2026. Typical server CPU list prices range from about USD 1,000 to more than USD 14,000 per socket (MK).

**Instinct GPUs (data center AI).** The family runs from MI300X/MI300A (2023) through MI325X (2024) and MI350X/MI355X (2025, CDNA 4, 288 GB HBM3E) to the MI400 series (MI455X for AI training and inference at scale, MI430X for HPC and sovereign AI with FP64 support, MI440X for enterprise). The MI400 series launched in 2026 with HBM4 (432 GB per GPU, MK). The MI500 is planned for 2027. AMD's differentiation has centred on memory capacity and bandwidth, which favour large-model inference, plus competitive total cost of ownership and an open ecosystem. Average selling prices are estimated at USD 15,000-35,000 per accelerator (MK, analyst estimates), and much more when sold as part of full racks.

**Helios rack-scale platform.** A 72-GPU MI450/MI455X rack with EPYC Venice CPUs, Pensando Vulcano NICs, UALink-over-Ethernet scale-up and Ultra Ethernet scale-out, built to the Open Compute Project Open Rack Wide standard. Helios is AMD's answer to Nvidia's NVL72 racks. It is the vehicle for the OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft and Oracle deployments, and it moves AMD from selling chips toward selling systems, with much larger deal sizes per customer (multi-billion-dollar, gigawatt-scale commitments).

**Networking and software.** Pensando DPUs and the Pollara 400G AI NIC (Vulcano 800G next) give AMD in-house networking for scale-out. ROCm is AMD's open-source GPU software stack. It is free, but critical to adoption, and "ROCm.ai" was released in 2026. Software maturity remains the most frequently cited gap against Nvidia CUDA, although support in PyTorch, JAX, vLLM, SGLang and Triton has improved materially.

### Client (30.7% of FY2025 revenue)

Ryzen desktop and notebook CPUs and APUs, including Ryzen AI (Copilot+ PC class with on-die NPUs), Ryzen AI Max/"Halo" for high-end mobile workstations, Ryzen PRO for commercial PCs, and Threadripper for workstations. AMD's X3D gaming CPUs (3D V-Cache) dominate the enthusiast desktop market. Client revenue grew 51% in FY2025 to USD 10,640 million and 22.5% YoY in Q2 FY2026 to USD 3,062 million, driven by share gains and richer mix. AM5 socket support was extended through 2029. Gorgon and Medusa are the next client families.

### Gaming (11.3% of FY2025 revenue)

The segment combines Radeon discrete GPUs (RDNA 4; Radeon RX 9000 series) with semi-custom SoCs for Sony PlayStation 5 and Microsoft Xbox Series consoles, as well as Valve's Steam Deck and other handhelds. Semi-custom is a low-margin, high-volume, design-win-driven business. Its revenue follows the console cycle; Q2 FY2026 Gaming revenue fell 30.6% YoY as the current console generation matures. AMD's position in the next console generation, including a reported next-generation Xbox partnership (MK), is a key long-run driver.

### Embedded (10.0% of FY2025 revenue)

Xilinx-heritage FPGAs (Virtex, Kintex, Artix, Spartan), adaptive SoCs (Zynq, Versal, including Versal Premium Gen 2 and Versal AI Edge), Alveo accelerator cards, Kria system-on-modules, and embedded x86 (EPYC Embedded, Ryzen Embedded, the new Ryzen AI Embedded X100). End markets are industrial, aerospace and defence, automotive, test and measurement, communications, broadcast and healthcare. Embedded is AMD's highest-margin, most diversified segment. It went through a long inventory correction (FY2025 revenue -3%) before recovering in 2026 (Q2 +18.6% YoY, segment operating margin 39.5%).

## 5. CUSTOMERS & GO-TO-MARKET

### Customer segments

1. **Hyperscalers and AI labs.** This is the fastest-growing and most concentrated group: Microsoft (Azure; Helios at scale; EPYC VMs), Meta (EPYC; MI300X/MI350; Helios), Oracle (OCI MI355X superclusters; MI450 commitment), OpenAI (6 GW agreement), Anthropic (up to 2 GW of MI450 in Helios), AWS and Google (EPYC instances), plus neoclouds (Tensorwave, Vultr, Crusoe, Cirrascale) and sovereign AI programmes (HUMAIN in Saudi Arabia, with a Cisco/HUMAIN joint venture targeting 1 GW by 2030). Deal structures now include multi-year, gigawatt-denominated commitments and, in OpenAI's case, equity warrants.
2. **Enterprise and HPC via OEMs.** Dell, HPE, Lenovo, Supermicro and Cisco servers carry EPYC and Instinct. HPE and AMD power exascale systems such as El Capitan and Frontier, and the planned "Herder" system uses MI430X and Venice.
3. **PC OEMs and channel.** HP, Lenovo, Dell, ASUS and Acer build Ryzen notebooks and desktops. Distributors and system integrators serve the DIY desktop market, where AMD holds a majority share of enthusiast CPU sales (MK).
4. **Semi-custom.** Sony and Microsoft, with long-cycle (5-7 year) design wins.
5. **Embedded.** Thousands of industrial, automotive, defence and communications customers, served largely through distribution (Avnet, Arrow and others, MK).

### Go-to-market model

AMD combines direct sales and co-engineering teams for hyperscalers and AI labs with OEM partnerships for enterprise and broad distribution for PC and embedded. The AI sales motion is consultative and long-cycle: proofs of concept, software porting with AMD engineers, model-specific optimization (for example day-zero support for major open models), then multi-quarter cluster deployments. The ZT Systems design team lets AMD deliver validated rack designs, which manufacturing partners such as Sanmina then build. That shortens customers' time-to-deployment, a gap AMD previously had against Nvidia's reference systems.

### Partnerships

- **TSMC:** leading-edge wafers (N3, N2) and CoWoS advanced packaging, with growing US (Arizona) production (MK).
- **GlobalFoundries:** mature-node wafers.
- **Memory suppliers:** SK hynix, Samsung and Micron supply HBM.
- **ATMP partners:** Tongfu Microelectronics joint ventures, SPIL and KYEC handle assembly and test. Purchases from equity-method investees were USD 594 million in Q2 FY2026.
- **Software ecosystem:** Hugging Face, PyTorch Foundation, Microsoft and Meta engineering collaborations, and Cerebras (a 2026 Helios inference collaboration).

### Case studies

- **OpenAI.** A 6 GW multi-generation commitment beginning with 1 GW of MI450 in the second half of 2026. This makes OpenAI a strategic co-design partner and, through the warrant, a potential shareholder.
- **Oracle.** Among the first to deploy MI355X at zettascale cluster sizes and committed to MI450 superclusters.
- **Microsoft.** Uses MI300X for production Azure OpenAI inference and expanded Helios deployment plans in 2026.
- **Exascale HPC.** El Capitan (LLNL, MI300A) and Frontier (ORNL, MI250X) were the world's top supercomputers (MK).

## 6. INDUSTRY OVERVIEW

### Definition and scope

AMD operates in the semiconductor industry (NAICS 334413, Semiconductor and Related Device Manufacturing; SIC 3674). Within it, AMD competes across several logic markets: data center compute (server CPUs, AI accelerators, networking), PC processors, discrete and semi-custom graphics, and programmable logic and embedded processing. The global semiconductor market was about USD 630 billion in 2024 and was estimated by WSTS at more than USD 700 billion for 2025, with forecasts approaching USD 1 trillion by about 2030 (MK; industry association estimates).

### Structure and growth

- **Data center AI accelerators.** This is the industry's growth engine. AMD estimates the data center AI accelerator market will exceed USD 500 billion by 2028 (June 2025 "Advancing AI" event, MK) and the broader data center compute TAM will reach about USD 1 trillion by 2030 (2025-11-11 Analyst Day). Nvidia dominates, with an estimated 80%-90% share of merchant GPU revenue. Its Q2 FY2027 (quarter ended July 2026) revenue was reported at USD 96.2 billion (Nvidia 8-K and press coverage, M confidence), more than eight times AMD's total quarterly revenue. Custom ASICs (Google TPU, AWS Trainium, Meta MTIA, OpenAI/Broadcom) are the second competitive vector.
- **Server CPUs.** A mature but re-accelerating market of roughly USD 30-40 billion a year (MK), structurally a two-vendor x86 market (Intel, AMD) now being entered by Arm-based designs (AWS Graviton, Google Axion, Microsoft Cobalt, Nvidia Grace/Vera, Ampere). Agentic AI and AI head-node demand drove a server CPU upcycle in 2025-2026.
- **PCs.** About 260-270 million units a year globally (MK), cyclical, with the Windows 10 end-of-support refresh (October 2025) and AI PCs supporting 2025-2026 demand. x86 remains dominant, with Arm (Qualcomm, Apple, and Nvidia/MediaTek entrants) a growing threat.
- **Gaming GPUs and consoles.** Cyclical and tied to console generations; Nvidia leads discrete GPUs with about 90% unit share (MK).
- **FPGAs and adaptive computing.** About USD 10-12 billion a year (MK). AMD (Xilinx) and Altera (now majority-owned by Silver Lake after Intel's 2025 stake sale, MK) form a duopoly, with Lattice and Microchip in low-end and mid-range parts.

### Key trends and drivers

1. **Hyperscale AI capex.** The top US hyperscalers' combined capex is expected to exceed USD 400-500 billion in 2026 (MK). This is the single largest demand driver, and also the largest cyclical risk.
2. **Shift to rack-scale and system-level competition.** Performance is increasingly decided at the rack and cluster level (interconnect, networking, power and cooling, software), favouring vendors with full-stack offerings.
3. **Inference over training.** Inference is growing as a share of AI compute. Memory capacity and cost per token become more important, which plays to AMD's HBM-heavy designs.
4. **Open standards.** UALink, Ultra Ethernet and OCP rack standards aim to reduce dependence on proprietary Nvidia NVLink/InfiniBand. AMD is a lead sponsor of each.
5. **Advanced packaging and HBM supply.** TSMC CoWoS capacity and HBM availability are the binding supply constraints for AI accelerators.
6. **Chiplets and leading-edge nodes.** TSMC N2 ramps in 2026. Cost per wafer is rising sharply, which favours designs that mix process nodes, an area where AMD has a lead.

### Regulatory environment

- **Export controls.** US restrictions on AI chip exports to China (October 2022, October 2023 and April 2025 licence requirements on MI308/H20-class parts), the shifting framework for AI diffusion, and possible revenue-sharing arrangements for licensed China sales all directly affect AMD. China, including Hong Kong, was historically a mid-teens percentage of AMD revenue by ship-to location (MK).
- **Tariffs and industrial policy.** Section 232 semiconductor tariff investigations and the CHIPS Act incentives for US manufacturing affect TSMC Arizona sourcing and costs.
- **Antitrust.** The x86 cross-licence with Intel survives a change of control only under specific terms (MK). Large AI partnerships, such as warrant-linked supply deals, may draw regulatory scrutiny.

### Industry dynamics (Porter view)

Barriers to entry are very high: billions in R&D per generation, leading-edge foundry access, IP portfolios and software ecosystems. Supplier power is high, because TSMC and the three HBM makers are concentrated. Buyer power is rising, because hyperscalers are large, sophisticated and able to design their own silicon. The threat of substitutes from custom ASICs is material. Rivalry is intense but concentrated among a few players.

### Positioning framework

On a two-axis view of breadth of compute portfolio versus AI ecosystem maturity:
- **Nvidia:** top-right (broad AI platform, most mature ecosystem).
- **AMD:** the broadest portfolio of any merchant vendor (x86 CPU, GPU, FPGA, DPU/NIC, racks), with second-tier but improving AI ecosystem maturity.
- **Intel:** broad portfolio, but behind on AI and on process execution.
- **Broadcom and Marvell:** narrow (custom ASIC and networking) but deeply embedded in hyperscalers.

### AMD's competitive advantages

1. **The only merchant vendor with leadership-class x86 CPUs and data center GPUs.** This allows CPU+GPU+NIC co-design and bundled selling to hyperscalers.
2. **Chiplet and advanced-packaging leadership.** Lower cost and faster iteration, with an annual cadence across EPYC and Instinct.
3. **Memory-capacity advantage in AI accelerators.** For example 288 GB HBM3E (MI355X) and 432 GB HBM4 (MI400) (MK). That suits large-model inference.
4. **The open-ecosystem positioning.** ROCm, UALink and Ultra Ethernet appeal to customers who want a second source and leverage against Nvidia pricing.
5. **Customer validation.** Multi-gigawatt commitments from OpenAI and Anthropic and deployments at Meta, Microsoft and Oracle reduce adoption risk.
6. **Balance sheet.** Net cash of about USD 9,885 million funds ecosystem investment and supply commitments.

### Competitive vulnerabilities

1. **The software gap.** CUDA's installed base and tooling remain the industry default. ROCm progress is real but trails, particularly for training at scale.
2. **Scale.** Nvidia's revenue is about eight times AMD's, which gives Nvidia far greater R&D, supply-chain priority (CoWoS, HBM) and ecosystem gravity.
3. **Networking.** Nvidia's NVLink and Spectrum-X are proven at scale. AMD's UALink-over-Ethernet and Vulcano are first-generation at rack scale.
4. **Customers building their own chips.** Custom ASICs cap the merchant GPU share available to AMD.
5. **Gaming and semi-custom cyclicality.** The current console cycle is maturing (Q2 FY2026 Gaming -30.6% YoY).

| Gaming (dGPU + semi-custom) | ~USD 25 billion | ~USD 30 billion | ~4% | #2 dGPU; #1 consoles |

| **Total** | **~USD 340-400 billion** | **~USD 840-960 billion** | **~20%** | |

### Serviceable market and share opportunity

AMD's serviceable addressable market (SAM) excludes captive custom-ASIC volume and China-restricted AI accelerators. Taking about 60%-70% of the AI accelerator TAM as merchant-addressable, SAM is roughly USD 600-700 billion by 2030 (estimate).

The management targets imply Data Center revenue compounding above 60% a year from the FY2025 base of USD 16,635 million. Three to five years at that rate takes Data Center revenue to about USD 68-175 billion (60% CAGR over 3 years gives about 68,000; over 5 years about 174,000). That range is wide and very sensitive to the timing of the MI450/MI500 ramps. Even the low end implies merchant AI accelerator share rising into the low-to-mid teens. Each 1 GW of deployed AI compute is commonly estimated to represent "double-digit billions" of USD of AMD revenue (management commentary around the OpenAI deal, MK). The combined 8 GW of OpenAI and Anthropic commitments therefore implies a multi-year backlog potentially above USD 100 billion if fully executed (estimate; deployment is milestone-dependent).

### Penetration strategy

1. **Anchor customers.** Land gigawatt-scale frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic) and hyperscalers (Meta, Microsoft, Oracle) on Helios to prove scale.
2. **Annual cadence.** MI350 (2025), MI400 (2026), MI500 (2027) keep pace with Nvidia's cadence.
3. **Systems.** Use ZT design capability plus manufacturing partners to sell turnkey racks.
4. **Software.** ROCm investment, acquisitions (Nod.ai, Silo AI, Brium, MEXT) and day-zero model support.
5. **CPU attach.** Lean on EPYC's incumbency to cross-sell GPUs and NICs.
6. **Sovereign and enterprise AI.** HUMAIN, Cisco and TCS partnerships, and the MI430X/MI440X for on-premises customers.

### Penetration rate today

AMD's FY2025 Data Center revenue (USD 16,635 million), set against an estimated USD 260-300 billion data center compute market (accelerators plus server CPUs plus networking, 2025), implies roughly 6% penetration. Management's targets require that figure to rise to about 12%-18% by 2030 (estimate).

## 9. RISK ASSESSMENT

### Company-specific risks

**1. AI accelerator execution risk (High severity / Medium likelihood).** The investment case and the current valuation (about 28.5x FY2025 revenue) depend on the MI450/Helios ramp from the second half of 2026 and on MI500 in 2027. Rack-scale systems combine new silicon, HBM4, new interconnect (UALink over Ethernet) and new networking (Vulcano). A slip of one or two quarters, or yield and thermal problems, could push billions of USD of revenue out of FY2026-2027 and damage credibility with the frontier labs. Mitigants: AMD's record of on-schedule launches, the ZT design team, and customer co-engineering.

**2. Customer concentration (High / Medium).** A handful of hyperscalers and AI labs now drive most Data Center growth. The OpenAI (6 GW) and Anthropic (2 GW) commitments are milestone-based and depend on those customers' own financing and compute plans. A slowdown or renegotiation by one anchor customer would remove a substantial share of projected growth. The warrant structure also means that OpenAI's deployment pace and AMD's share price are linked. Mitigants: diversification across Meta, Microsoft, Oracle, sovereigns and neoclouds.

**3. Key-person dependency (Medium / Low).** Lisa Su (CEO since 2014) is closely identified with AMD's turnaround and strategy, and much of the senior technical leadership has been in place for more than a decade. An unplanned departure would likely cause a sharp de-rating. Mitigants: a deep bench (Papermaster, Norrod, Hu) and a strong engineering culture, though no named successor has been disclosed.

**4. Software ecosystem gap (Medium / High).** ROCm still trails CUDA in breadth, maturity and developer mindshare, particularly for large-scale training and new model architectures. If customers face higher porting costs, AMD's effective price-performance advantage shrinks and it may be limited to inference or to price-sensitive deployments. Mitigants: open-source momentum, hyperscaler co-development, and acquisitions.

**5. Supply-chain and foundry concentration (High / Low-Medium).** Virtually all leading-edge AMD products are made by TSMC in Taiwan, with CoWoS packaging and HBM from three suppliers. Capacity allocation favours the largest buyer (Nvidia). Any disruption in Taiwan would be severe. Inventory of USD 8,468 million on 2026-06-27 and growing purchase commitments raise write-down risk if demand shifts. Mitigants: TSMC Arizona, multiple HBM vendors, and long-term supply agreements.

**6. Acquisition and intangible-impairment risk (Medium / Low).** Goodwill of USD 25,470 million and intangibles of USD 15,635 million (together 48.7% of total assets) are mostly from Xilinx. Weak Embedded performance or deal integration issues (ZT, MEXT) could trigger impairments. The Sanmina earn-out receivable (USD 348 million) is subject to impairment review each period.

### Industry and market risks

**7. Competitive intensity from Nvidia (High / High).** Nvidia's scale (quarterly revenue of about USD 96 billion), its annual cadence (Blackwell Ultra, then Vera Rubin, then Rubin Ultra) and its full-stack offering may limit AMD's share or force price concessions. Nvidia can also bundle networking and software at prices AMD struggles to match. Mitigants: customers' strategic desire for a second source, and AMD's memory-capacity advantage.

**8. Custom ASIC substitution (Medium-High / Medium).** Google, AWS, Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI (with Broadcom) are scaling in-house accelerators that can be cheaper for their own workloads. Growth in ASIC share shrinks the merchant TAM AMD can address. Mitigants: ASICs are less flexible across fast-changing model architectures, and AMD can offer custom variants.

**9. Arm encroachment in CPUs (Medium / Medium).** Arm-based hyperscaler CPUs (Graviton, Axion, Cobalt), Nvidia's Grace/Vera and Qualcomm's PC chips threaten the x86 duopoly that underpins EPYC and Ryzen. Mitigants: x86 software compatibility, AMD's performance lead and the x86 Ecosystem Advisory Group (formed with Intel in 2024, MK).

**10. Export controls and trade policy (High / Medium).** US restrictions already caused about USD 440 million of net FY2025 charges (MI308) and excluded AMD from most of the China AI accelerator market. Further rules, Chinese retaliation (for example antitrust probes or rare-earth controls), or Section 232 semiconductor tariffs could hit revenue and margins. Mitigants: limited China AI exposure today, and US-based production options.

### Financial risks

**11. Margin dilution from the rack-scale mix (Medium / Medium).** Selling full racks includes pass-through content (memory, networking and mechanical components) at lower gross margin than chips. Non-GAAP gross margin was about 56% in Q2 FY2026, and early Helios ramp costs could weigh on it. The over-35% non-GAAP operating margin target requires strong operating leverage on R&D (USD 2,528 million in Q2 FY2026, up 33.5% YoY). Mitigants: mix shift away from semi-custom, and pricing power in EPYC.

**12. Shareholder dilution and stock-based compensation (Medium / High).** SBC was USD 1,638 million in FY2025 (4.7% of revenue). The OpenAI warrant for up to 160 million shares, about 9.8% of the 1,632 million outstanding (MK), would dilute existing holders if it vests, even though vesting is tied to value-creating milestones. Diluted share count rose from 1,630 million (Q2 FY2025) to 1,659 million (Q2 FY2026). Buybacks were paused in Q2 FY2026 (six-month repurchases USD 221 million).

**13. Off-balance-sheet commitments (Medium / Low).** The Q2 FY2026 10-Q discloses up to USD 4.1 billion of maximum exposure from guarantees on commercial partners' data center lease obligations (terms up to 15 years) and up to USD 5.0 billion of new investment commitments after quarter-end. These support ecosystem build-out but expose AMD to the credit risk of neoclouds and partners.

### Macroeconomic risks

**14. AI capex cyclicality (High / Medium).** AMD's growth is tied to hyperscaler and AI-lab capex, which depends on AI monetization, capital-market appetite and energy availability. A capex pause, as happened in past semiconductor cycles, would hit Data Center revenue and inventory hard. Semiconductors are among the most cyclical sectors, and AMD's revenue fell 3.9% in FY2023.

**15. Consumer and PC cyclicality (Medium / Medium).** Client and Gaming (42.0% of FY2025 revenue) depend on consumer spending, PC replacement cycles and the console generation. Higher memory prices in 2026 (driven by AI demand for DRAM and HBM, MK) could raise PC bill-of-materials costs and dampen unit demand.

**16. Geopolitical and FX exposure (High severity / Low likelihood for Taiwan; Medium for FX).** Most revenue is billed in USD, but operating costs in India, Canada, Europe and China and the Asian supply base create FX sensitivity. AMD hedges with forwards (USD 3.2 billion of cash-flow-hedge notional on 2026-06-27). Cross-strait tension is the most severe tail risk for the entire fabless industry.

*Note: 16 risks are listed to cover all four categories in depth. Risks 1, 2, 7, 10, 12 and 14 are the six most material for the investment thesis.*
